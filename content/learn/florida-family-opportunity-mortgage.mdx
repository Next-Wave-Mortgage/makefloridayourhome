Buying a home for an aging parent or an adult child with a disability usually means investor pricing and a large down payment, unless you use the Family Opportunity Mortgage.

This guide explains who qualifies, how much you need down, how lenders count your income when you're paying for two homes, and what changes when the home is in Florida.

You'll come away knowing whether your situation fits, what the loan will cost compared with a second home or investment loan, and the documents to have ready.

## Family Opportunity Mortgage at a Glance

- **What it is:** a Fannie Mae rule (Freddie Mac has a similar one) that treats a home you buy for a parent or disabled adult child as your primary residence
- **Down payment:** usually 5%, compared with at least 10% for a second home and 15% for a single-family investment property
- **Rates:** owner-occupied pricing, the lowest conventional rates available
- **Credit:** 620 or higher at most lenders
- **Who can live there:** your parent, or your adult child with a disability who can't work or doesn't have enough income to qualify on their own
- **Rules:** no rent, no lease, and the occupant must live there as their primary residence

## Who Qualifies

The rule covers two situations. Your lender will document the relationship and why the person living in the home can't buy it on their own.

| Situation | Covered? | What the Lender Needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buying for an elderly parent | Yes | Your parent can't work or doesn't have enough income to qualify for a mortgage |
| Buying for a disabled adult child | Yes | Your child has a disability and can't work or doesn't have enough income to qualify |
| Buying for a college student | No | Use a second home loan, or have the student buy with you as a non-occupying co-borrower |
| Buying for a sibling or other relative | No | Financed as a second home or investment property |
| Renting the home to family | No | Any rent makes it an investment property |

A parent or legal guardian can use the rule for an adult child with a disability. If your child receives SSI or SSDI, an award letter or doctor's statement usually documents the disability. Our guide to [Florida home loans and grants for disabled buyers](/learn/florida-housing-grants-for-disabled) covers options when your child wants to buy in their own name.

## Family Opportunity vs. Second Home vs. Investment Loan

Without this rule, a home for a family member is financed as a second home or investment property. Here's what that difference costs.

| Feature | Family Opportunity | Second Home | Investment Property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum down payment | 5% | 10% | 15% (single-family) |
| Interest rate | Owner-occupied pricing | Slightly higher | Highest, with added pricing fees |
| Gift funds allowed | Yes | Yes | No |
| Who lives there | Parent or disabled adult child | You, part of the year | Tenant |
| Rent allowed | No | No | Yes |

On a $350,000 Florida home, 5% down is $17,500. An investment loan would need at least $52,500 down, plus a higher rate on the whole loan.

## How Lenders Count Your Income

You're paying for two homes, so the lender adds your current housing payment to the new one.

- **Your current home:** your mortgage or rent, taxes, insurance, and HOA dues all count
- **The new home:** principal, interest, taxes, homeowners and flood insurance, HOA dues, and mortgage insurance
- **Other debts:** car loans, student loans, and credit card minimums
- **The limit:** total debts generally can't exceed 45% to 50% of your gross monthly income

**Example:** You earn $12,000 a month, pay $2,800 for your own home, and have a $500 car payment. At a 50% debt-to-income ratio, your debts can total $6,000 a month, which leaves $2,700 for your parent's home. If Florida taxes, insurance, and mortgage insurance run about $700 a month, that leaves about $2,000 for principal and interest. At 6.5% over 30 years, that supports a loan of about $316,000, or a home price of about $333,000 with 5% down.

Your parent's Social Security or pension income can't be used to qualify unless your parent is also a borrower on the loan.

## Florida Rules That Affect Your Costs

- **Property taxes:** If the home is only in your name, it's taxed as non-homestead property, with no homestead exemption and no Save Our Homes cap. Non-homestead assessments can still only rise 10% a year for non-school taxes. If your parent is on the title and lives there, they may qualify for homestead and the [senior property tax exemptions](/learn/florida-property-tax-exemptions-for-seniors). Talk to your title company before closing about how to take title.
- **Insurance:** Florida homeowners and wind coverage can add several hundred dollars a month, and lenders require flood insurance in high-risk flood zones. Get quotes before you make an offer.
- **Condos and 55+ communities:** Condos must meet conventional building approval rules, and many Florida buildings don't because of reserve, inspection, or litigation issues. See our [Florida condo loan guide](/home-loan/condo-loan) before you shop. Age-restricted 55+ communities are eligible.
- **Accessibility upgrades:** Ramps, grab bars, and walk-in showers can be paid for after closing, or financed with a renovation loan if the home needs bigger changes.

## Documents to Have Ready

- Your paystubs, W-2s or tax returns, bank statements, and current mortgage statement
- Proof of relationship, such as a birth certificate
- A letter explaining that your parent or child will live in the home and why they can't qualify on their own
- Your parent's Social Security or pension award letter, or your child's disability documentation
- A signed statement that the home won't be rented

## How to Buy a Home for Your Parent in Florida

1. **Get pre-approved:** Your lender confirms your income can carry both homes and that the occupancy exception applies.
2. **Decide on title:** Choose whether the home is in your name only or whether your parent or child is added, which affects homestead and estate planning.
3. **Shop with a budget for Florida costs:** Include insurance, flood zone, and HOA or condo approval before you make an offer.
4. **Submit the occupancy documents:** Your lender reviews the relationship, income, and occupancy letters during underwriting.
5. **Close and move your family member in:** The home must become their primary residence, typically within 60 days of closing.
